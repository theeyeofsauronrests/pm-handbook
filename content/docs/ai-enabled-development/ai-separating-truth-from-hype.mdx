---
title: "“AI”: Separating Truth from Hype"
description: A practical guide to rules, statistics, machine learning, and LLMs.
---

Much of what is sold as “AI” is neither new nor particularly intelligent.

PMs do not need to design models, but they do need to distinguish a useful technique from a marketing label attached to uncertainty.

A useful question is: **What does the system do when it is wrong?**

If a vendor, stakeholder, or prototype cannot answer that question clearly, the team does not understand the product well enough to ship it.

## Different tools solve different problems

- **Rules engines:** explicit `if this, then that` logic. Predictable, explainable, boring, and often correct.
- **Statistics:** patterns, thresholds, regressions, and probabilities. A surprising amount of “AI” stops here.
- **Supervised machine learning:** predictions learned from labeled examples.
- **Unsupervised machine learning:** clustering or anomaly detection without predefined labels.
- **Large language models:** fluent language generation and transformation. Fluency is not truth.

Machine learning is not inherently better than rules. It is usually harder to reason about.

## Common claims and less exciting reality

- “Automatically identifies threats” may mean a classifier trained on historical examples.
- “AI-powered decision support” may be a ranking heuristic in a nicer dashboard.
- “Learns over time” may mean someone manually retrains it every quarter.
- “Reduces operator workload” may mean operators now check both the model and the original data.

None of those approaches are automatically bad. Imprecise language is.

## When ML makes sense

ML is useful when patterns are too complex for rules, data is sufficient and representative, and approximate outputs can still create value.

It is risky when data quality is poor, the environment changes faster than the model, errors are rare but catastrophic, or decisions must be fully explainable.

## LLM reality check

LLMs are good at summarization, drafting, format translation, and assisting humans.

They are bad at being reliably correct, respecting hidden constraints, and knowing when they do not know.

Operational use should be bounded, clearly advisory where appropriate, monitored, and designed to fail safely.

For PM-specific guidance, see [Responsible LLM Use for PMs](/docs/ai-enabled-development/responsible-llm-use-for-pms).

## PM questions

- What happens when it is wrong?
- How often is it wrong?
- How would we know?
- Who owns the decision?
- Could rules solve most of the problem more safely?
- Are we adding intelligence, or opacity?

“AI” is not a product strategy.

<PMTakeaway>
Skepticism is not anti-innovation. It is how useful automation avoids becoming an expensive way to hide uncertainty.
</PMTakeaway>
