---
title: Stories, Bugs, and Spikes
description: Practical guidance for defining different kinds of work.
---

Tickets are product artifacts.

They are how intent, context, and constraints move through the system, especially when the team is distributed or working asynchronously. They do not have to be awful.

A good ticket communicates why, not just what. It enables autonomy without pretending to eliminate every question.

The right standard is **just enough, just in time**.

Too much detail ages badly and can over-prescribe the solution. Too little context creates rework and argument. Old tickets should be reviewed before they are estimated or assigned because assumptions decay.

<LLMLeverage>
Use an LLM to organize messy notes into the six-question ticket shape, compare a draft against known acceptance criteria, or point out where intent, risk, impact, and exclusions are missing.

Do not turn a single sentence into a ticket with a prompt and call it product work. You are paid to think. If you hand the thinking to a model, you are still accountable for the decision, but now with worse visibility into why the ticket says what it says.
</LLMLeverage>

Different ticket types serve different purposes. Do not force every kind of work into a user-story template.

## Stories

Stories describe a small slice of user-facing behavior.

A useful story answers:

1. What are we doing?
2. Why are we doing it?
3. What are we not doing?
4. What context is required?
5. How do we know it is done?
6. What questions remain?

Stories should fit within an iteration and leave implementation choices to Design and Engineering where appropriate.

If the "why" is unclear, priority debates will follow. If exclusions are unnamed, scope creep will write itself.

Classic "As a user..." templates are fine when they help. They become nonsense when they describe a task instead of a goal.

Nobody goes home satisfied because they submitted a form. What did they accomplish? Why did that action matter?

## Bugs

A bug describes a deviation from expected behavior.

Include:

- Minimal steps to reproduce
- Expected behavior
- Actual behavior
- Environment or relevant conditions
- Screenshots, logs, or response details when useful
- User, operational, or business impact

A good bug describes reality, not emotion. It should not be used to explore new functionality, capture vague dissatisfaction, or sneak in scope.

## Spikes

A spike is a time-boxed learning activity used when uncertainty blocks progress.

Every spike needs:

- A specific question
- A time limit
- A tangible deliverable
- A decision the work will inform

There must be a deliverable. "Research" without a decision or output is not a spike.

Learning is the deliverable, but only when the learning changes what the team does next.

## Decision quality

Every ticket should make the decision context easier to see.

For meaningful work, include:

- What is known
- What is assumed
- What would change the plan
- Whether the decision is reversible
- What scope is intentionally excluded

This does not mean every ticket needs a long template. It means tickets should not hide uncertainty behind confident phrasing.

## Working ticket signals

Tickets are working when:

- The PM understands the value and knows where to find answers.
- Design has enough context to solve the user problem without being boxed into one answer.
- Engineering has enough context to solve the technical problem without being treated like an implementation service.
- The team debates the best implementation, not the basic intent.

## Cut scope before quality

When time gets tight, cut scope before cutting correctness.

Ask:

- What is the smallest complete version that still creates value?
- Can this be tested manually before it is automated?
- What can be deferred without making the delivered behavior misleading?
- If this does not finish inside the appetite, will we cut it or extend it?

Shipping an incomplete slice of a larger idea is usually worse than shipping a smaller complete idea.
