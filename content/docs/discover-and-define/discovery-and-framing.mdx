---
title: Discovery and Framing
description: How PMs turn ambiguity into product direction without pretending uncertainty is gone.
---

Discovery is the work of reducing uncertainty enough to make the next decision.

It is not a ritual phase, a research theater, or a way to delay commitment until everyone feels safe. Good discovery turns a vague ask into a clearer frame: user, problem, constraints, tradeoffs, risks, and what not to build.

Modern [product discovery](https://www.producttalk.org/product-discovery/) is a shared mindset across Product, Design, and Engineering. It works when the team learns together before committing to a solution shape.

At its best, discovery is continuous. Teams should not make long strings of decisions without letting users, evidence, prototypes, or working software interrupt their assumptions.

## Start with the struggling moment

Do not start with the feature.

Start with the moment where someone is trying to accomplish something and gets stuck. The more specific the moment, the better the framing.

Ask:

- Who is the person or team?
- What are they trying to do?
- What happens right before the problem appears?
- What workaround exists today?
- Why has this not already been solved?
- Is this a user problem, a business problem, or both?

If the problem can only be described in abstract nouns, it is probably not framed yet.

## Separate truth from hypothesis

Discovery work should make confidence visible.

Use three buckets:

- **Known:** evidence the team trusts
- **Assumed:** beliefs that are plausible but unproven
- **Unknown:** questions that could change the direction

Do not flatten these into one polished narrative. A confident-sounding artifact that hides uncertainty is worse than a rough artifact that names it.

## Bound the playground

When uncertainty is high, it is often more useful to define boundaries than to force a crisp vision.

Good boundaries include:

- Users or contexts in scope
- Constraints that cannot move
- Risks the team will not accept
- Existing assets, tools, services, or patterns to consider
- Time or effort appetite
- Signals that would cause the team to pivot, continue, or stop

Inside those boundaries, the team can explore options without pretending it already knows the answer.

## Share the work across disciplines

Discovery is strongest when Product, Design, and Engineering each bring a different kind of risk into view.

**Product** frames the problem and priority:

- What problem are we solving, and why now?
- What user, business, or operational outcome matters?
- Which constraints shape the decision?
- What should be built, bought, adapted, simplified, or stopped?

**Design** makes the workflow tangible:

- What does the user actually do today?
- Where does friction or cognitive load appear?
- What is the simplest interaction the user would expect?
- Which assumptions can be tested with a prototype or workflow sketch?

**Engineering** tests feasibility and system impact:

- What data, integration, security, reliability, or performance constraints matter?
- What is the lowest-risk path to learn?
- What dependencies would this introduce?
- What would make this expensive to operate or hard to change?

Discovery fails when one discipline writes the answer and asks the others to decorate, approve, or implement it.

## Use artifacts to force decisions

A Blog / FAQ, prototype, workflow sketch, or spike should answer a question.

Useful artifacts create motion:

- They expose disagreement.
- They reveal missing constraints.
- They let Design and Engineering react.
- They help users or stakeholders misunderstand the idea in useful ways.
- They change what the team does next.

If an artifact does not change a decision, it may only be documentation.

<LLMLeverage>
Use an LLM before a discovery conversation to list assumptions, draft interview prompts, reframe vague asks into sharper problem statements, or generate counterarguments the team should consider.

Do not confuse that with evidence. The model has not watched a user struggle, felt the politics in the room, or learned which constraint is real. Treat its output as a thinking aid, not a source of truth.
</LLMLeverage>

## Keep users in the loop

Discovery does not require a giant research program.

Useful tactics include interviews, usability tests, assumption tests, journey maps, workflow sketches, opportunity trees, demand tests, and prototypes. The tactic matters less than the operating rhythm: the team should regularly expose its assumptions to people, evidence, or realistic use.

The gap between how the team talks about a product and how users experience it is where discovery earns its keep.

## Pick signals before solutions

Before committing to a solution, decide which signals will matter.

Useful signals can include:

- User behavior
- Workflow friction
- Error rate
- Support load
- Delivery risk
- Cost behavior
- Adoption quality

For more on metrics as decision inputs, see [Metrics and Signals](/docs/discover-and-define/metrics-and-signals).

## Bottom line

Discovery is not about proving the original idea was right. It is about learning what is true enough to decide what to do next.

<PMTakeaway>
Frame the next decision before you frame the whole product. Ask what signal would change the plan.
</PMTakeaway>
