---
title: Build, Reuse, or Buy
description: How PMs choose the right starting point without treating reuse as a universal answer.
---

Every product decision has a starting point.

Sometimes the right move is to build something new. Sometimes it is to reuse an existing capability. Sometimes it is to buy, integrate, configure, simplify, or stop.

Reuse is one option. It is not the center of product management.

## Start with the problem

Do not start by asking whether the team can reuse something.

Start by asking:

- What problem are we solving?
- Who has the problem?
- What constraints matter?
- How much certainty do we have?
- What would make the team change direction?
- What existing options are credible?

Only then compare paths.

## Compare the paths

**Build** when the problem is specific, differentiated, poorly served by existing options, or still needs learning before standardizing.

**Reuse** when an existing capability fits the workflow, has a clear owner, and reduces risk without forcing the wrong product shape.

**Buy or integrate** when the problem is common, the market already solves it well, and the long-term operating cost is acceptable.

**Stop or simplify** when the value does not justify the cost, risk, or complexity.

## Reuse still has carrying cost

An existing capability can reduce time, improve consistency, and avoid local invention.

It can also create constraints:

- The workflow may need to bend around it.
- The interface may not match the product need.
- The owner may have different priorities.
- Upgrades may create coordination work.
- Consumers may inherit failure modes they do not control.

The question is not only, "Can we reuse this?"

It is, "Are we willing to accept what comes with it?"

## Existing capabilities are products

When a team depends on an existing capability, PMs should treat it like a product relationship.

PMs should ask:

- Who consumes it?
- Who maintains it?
- What decisions does it remove?
- What decisions does it force?
- What breaks when it changes?
- How are issues handled?
- What is the adoption cost?
- What is the exit path?

If ownership is unclear, reuse may just move risk out of sight.

That is a systems problem, not a tooling detail. Reuse can make one team's delivery look faster while increasing another team's support burden, roadmap pressure, or change-management cost. The PM should ask where the risk went, not just whether the local estimate got smaller.

## Interfaces matter

Interfaces are where the tradeoff becomes real.

The interface may be a component API, data contract, service boundary, design pattern, deployment path, or working agreement.

Weak interfaces transfer confusion downstream. Strong interfaces reduce coordination cost.

## When reuse is wrong

Prefer building or experimenting when:

- The problem is still poorly understood
- The existing capability would force the wrong workflow
- Adoption cost is higher than duplication cost
- The abstraction would become more complex than the use case
- The team needs to learn before standardizing

Standardize after the shape is understood. Standardizing too early spreads the wrong decision.

## PM responsibilities

PMs do not need to make the technical decision alone. They do need to make the product tradeoff visible:

- What path are we choosing?
- What are we gaining?
- What are we giving up?
- How reversible is it?
- Who owns the consequences later?

## Bottom line

Do not worship greenfield work. Do not worship reuse. Choose the starting point that fits the problem, constraints, and evidence.

<PMTakeaway>
Reuse is useful when it fits. It is harmful when it becomes the answer before the problem is understood.
</PMTakeaway>
