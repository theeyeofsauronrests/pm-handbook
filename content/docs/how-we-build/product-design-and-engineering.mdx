---
title: Product, Design, and Engineering
description: How Product, Design, and Engineering preserve accountability while using friction well.
---

Product, Design, and Engineering should overlap early.

That does not mean the roles collapse into one another. The overlap matters because each discipline sees a different kind of risk.

## Distinct accountability

- **Product** owns viability: should this exist, and under what constraints?
- **Design** owns usability: can people understand and operate it correctly?
- **Engineering** owns feasibility and robustness: can it be built, integrated, secured, and sustained?

Good teams blur collaboration. They do not blur accountability.

Another useful frame is:

- **Valuable:** users or customers have a reason to choose it.
- **Usable:** people can understand and operate it.
- **Feasible:** the team can build and integrate it with the available technology and skills.
- **Viable:** the solution works within budgetary, legal, ethical, operational, and stakeholder constraints.

If one of those is missing, the product is not ready. It may be a good idea, but it is not yet a good product decision.

## Friction is signal

Cross-functional friction is not automatically waste.

A designer who pushes back on the workflow may be showing that the problem is not understood. An engineer who says a proposal is risky may be revealing a deeper constraint. A PM who narrows scope may be protecting reliability rather than lowering ambition.

The goal is not to remove friction. The goal is to use it before the cost of changing direction gets high.

## Preparation is not collaboration

A PM can use an LLM to draft user flows, summarize constraints, or simulate critique before a working session.

That can be useful preparation. It is not a substitute for a designer, engineer, user, or stakeholder reacting to the work.

Generated critique tends to stay inside the PM's frame. Real collaboration breaks the frame.

## Better handoffs

Handoffs are weakest when they pass polished answers across discipline boundaries.

They are stronger when they pass:

- The problem being solved
- Known constraints
- Open questions
- Decisions already made
- Decisions still needed
- Risks that should change scope

Artifacts should make the conversation easier. They should not pretend the conversation already happened.

Design handoff is not just a designer handing pictures to engineers.

It is the trio agreeing that the intended behavior is valuable, usable, feasible, viable, reliable, performant, and maintainable enough to build. Tools can help, but the real handoff is shared understanding.

## Bottom line

Bring Product, Design, and Engineering into the problem early enough that each discipline can change the shape of the work.

<PMTakeaway>
If collaboration never changes the plan, it probably was not collaboration. It was review.
</PMTakeaway>
